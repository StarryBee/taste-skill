Garden District · Dispatch

The Enchanted Cliffside Garden: Thirteen Lanterns With No Flame.

Witnesses describe amber light igniting without source at dusk. The mushrooms, they insist, were pulsing.

Residents of Mosshollow Bluff awoke to find the cliffside garden ablaze with amber light — yet no torch, no tallow, no gas lamp had been lit by any human hand. The wildflowers, improbably in bloom three weeks past their season, had turned their faces toward the light as though it were the sun.

Correspondent E. Ashveil, who reached the bluff before dawn on the third morning of the event, filed a single photograph before her camera ceased to function. That photograph is reproduced above. She noted in her dispatch: "The jar at the base of the post appeared to contain preserved light. Not a candle flame. Light itself."

By E. Ashveil · Garden District Bureau Section B · Page 3

Opinion & Analysis · Archive Division

Crowley and the Death of the Black Raven: What the Manuscript Really Says.

Our senior archivist has spent three weeks with the text. Her conclusions are not reassuring.

The manuscript arrived wrapped in black silk, sealed with purple wax bearing an insignia our most senior archivist could not immediately place. It weighed more than a book of its apparent size should. It smelled of iron.

"Crowley — Cái Chết Của Quạ Đen" — which translates as "The Death of the Black Raven" — is attributed to an author who signs themselves only as Ryu Vang. No such person appears in any provincial registry. The handwriting in the marginal annotations, however, is consistent across three separate exemplars held in our archive — meaning the same hand has been correcting this text for at least sixty years.

What makes the text remarkable is not its prose style. It is the specificity. Dates are given. Locations described in enough detail to be mapped against the current province. A chapter entitled "The Night of the Thirteen Lanterns" describes an event our garden correspondent independently reported this fortnight — six years before her dispatch was filed.

"The manuscript does not merely describe events. It anticipates them. Three of the passages we verified had not yet occurred at the time of this printing."

— Senior Archivist D. Crowne, Gazette Archive Division

Field Report · Nature & Omens

The Autumn Oracle speaks its last season.

Botanical records confirm two prior episodes of retention — both times, the tree shed everything at once, on a single windless night, into the water below. The water turned red for a week.

Provincial botanist Dr. Seara Holst examined the tree on the twelfth day. Her preliminary report noted "no observable signs of disease, drought stress, or unusual hormonal activity" — and then, on page seven, a single handwritten addition: "The tree appears aware of being observed."

The village elders, when asked to comment, declined. Separately, they began building on higher ground.

By T. Halvane · Waterfront District Section D · Page 4
